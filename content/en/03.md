Finding Kennet was a coincidence. Two months had passed since Robert left the canyon. He was looking for materials in the hills to the north-west, among the remains of an old mining town, when he saw a man half buried in the sand. He was huge, as broad-shouldered as a wardrobe, about thirty years old. His lips were cracked with thirst, and his eyes had turned cloudy and fixed like a dead man's.

Robert tied him to his bike, carried him to his shelter and for three days gave him water with a spoon. On the fourth day the man spoke.

"Kennet," he said. "I ran away from the Great State. Long before the Catastrophe. I didn't want to fight their war."

He had untidy dark chestnut hair, and in his eyes a kindness that did not match his size at all. He was an expert tracker: he read the stars, sensed rain in the sand and danger in the wind. When he heard Robert's plan he was silent for a long time, then simply asked, "Where do we start?" From that day on he was Robert's right hand.

Sasha arrived a month later. She was a red-haired woman with a sharp gaze, whose thin fingers were always playing with a pencil. Before the Catastrophe she had been an accountant. In the shelter where she lived she had heard a talk about Our Utopia on the radio: the call Robert broadcast into the desert every evening, not knowing who was listening. She turned up at the gate with a notebook and a calculator.

"Your dreams are lovely," she told Robert on the first day. "But the difference between a dream and survival is the difference between two columns of a table. Water, grain, fuel, people. Give me those, and I'll give you a fortress."

Kennet laughed at her bluntness. Sasha, though, looked at Kennet a moment too long, and nobody noticed. Not even Kennet.

The plans of the fortress were drawn by **Luis**. He was thirty-three, with long hair and a carefully trimmed beard, and even in the hardest times he wore a light-coloured shirt and a colourful scarf tied elegantly around his neck. Before the Catastrophe he had been an architect. In the way he spoke, walked, even held his tea, there was the refinement of a vanished world. He said very little about himself; only once had he mentioned that he came from the south, "from a country where nobody talks about anything but football and politics". He was the one who taught everyone to drink mate.

The fortress Luis drew was pentagonal. It would sit on a hill, protected by two rings of walls. In the outer ring, cylindrical watchtowers with narrow windows; in the middle of the inner ring, a tall, slender main tower overlooking the whole valley, and a wide square for meetings and festivals. Because the hill was steep and rugged, it was hard for vehicles to approach; only someone who really wanted to come could reach the gate.

"And it will be turquoise," said Luis. "Like a sea in the middle of the desert. People should see it from kilometres away. Like a lighthouse."

"Enemies will see it too," said Kennet.

"Enemies see everything," said Luis with a shrug. "People who hope only see the bright things."

The building took two years. People who heard Robert's broadcasts came one by one, then in groups: farmers, welders, teachers, former soldiers. Among them was **Martín**: a former soldier, a master of the martial arts, quick to decide but also quick to anger. And **Jeni**: a charismatic woman with short black hair, a big nose, a wide mouth and sharp features, who turned every conversation into a negotiation. Before the Catastrophe she had been an economist.

One morning Jeni planted herself in front of Robert.

"There's too much chaos in the fortress. We need a currency. We have to measure labour and resources."

"Money and greed made the world what it is, Jeni," said Robert. "Here everyone works and everyone shares. No money."

Jeni smiled; that smile chilled Robert to the bone. Two days later whispers began to go round the fortress: Robert was a dreamer, Robert didn't know how to govern, Robert would let everyone starve. A month later a quarter of the population left the fortress with Jeni, to build "their own realistic utopia".

That night Robert could not sleep. From his window he looked down at the emptying courtyards. There were women in the fortress; beautiful, brave, clever women. Some smiled at him, some wanted to sit next to him at dinner. Robert treated them all politely, but with the politeness of a wall. *They all leave*, he told himself. *My mother, my first love, the ones after... Now even Jeni has left me. And her, the woman in the canyon, rejected me before I even opened the door.* A woman's tears no longer moved him. He felt no pain for any woman; he had forced himself to get used to that.

Even so, sometimes at midnight he took the little desert rose out of his drawer and turned it over in his palm. Then he got angry with himself and put it back.

One night Kennet knocked at the door.

"You can't sleep," he said. It was not a question.

"The fortress is finished, Kennet. The walls are up, the towers are done. But inside it feels empty."

Kennet went to the window and looked into the darkness for a while.

"Building the walls was easy," he said. "Now comes the hard part. Building the people. And maybe... yourself."

Robert did not answer. He squeezed the desert rose in his palm; the sharp edges of the crystal dug into his hand.
