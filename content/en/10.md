The army halted in the afternoon at the foot of the hill. Kennet counted them through his binoculars, and the more he counted, the paler he grew.

"Six times our number," he said. "Maybe more. Motorbikes, armoured trucks. And look at that."

Above the army, little black dots were hovering, humming like a swarm of bees. Drones. Each one carried an explosive. In the middle of the army two flags were flying: the coiled snake on a yellow background and the golden gate on a black background. Juan, with his necklace of bones, stood on the first armoured truck. Beside him, his hand bandaged, was Martín.

Nobody in the fortress said anything. There was nothing to say.

They carried Robert to the square in a chair. His fever was still high; Anselmo had stitched his wound again, grumbling, "If you open it again I'll shoot you like a horse and sell your hide." Robert stood up. His knees were shaking, but he held himself up.

"Women, children and the old to the inner ring," he said. "To the cellars of the main tower. Those who can fight, with me to the walls."

Nobody moved. Then a woman's voice rose from the crowd. One of Rosa's rebels, a mother who had lost both her sons in the mines of Necrology.

"I'm not going down to the cellar! I escaped from the mines, I'm not going underground again!"

Another:

"Neither am I!"

And another. And another. Old men, young girls, fourteen-year-old boys. In the end only the babies, their mothers and the very oldest went down to the cellar. Everyone else climbed the walls. With rifles, bows, stones and pots of boiling oil.

Robert turned to Rosa.

"You go to the inner ring too, Rosa. Please."

Rosa looked at him as she had looked at him that time in the canyon, by the well: measuring, weighing, and this time with an infinite tenderness.

"I chose you in the square, Robert, in front of the whole fortress. Choosing isn't leaving; it's staying. You taught me that."

Robert wanted to say something. He could not. He only took her hand and did not let it go.

The attack began at sunset.

The drones came first. They exploded over the turquoise walls; the stones cracked and the top of one of the towers was wrapped in flames. Kennet in the north tower with his rocket launcher and Sasha in the south tower with her rifle fired without stopping. It was said that Sasha carefully calculated every bullet before firing it; that night she did not miss once.

Then the motorbikes began to climb the hill. The steep slopes Luis had drawn slowed them down; from above, the people of the fortress rained stones, boiling oil and arrows on them. The first wave broke. So did the second. Shouts of joy rose from the fortress.

But Juan was patient. And Martín knew the fortress's secrets.

At midnight an explosion came from the west. The drainage tunnel. Martín had sent a hundred of the army's best men in through Luis's secret passage. At the same time an armoured truck smashed at full speed into the main gate of the outer ring. The gate groaned, buckled and collapsed.

The savages poured into the outer ring like a flood.

The defenders fell back to the inner ring. Robert, Rosa, Tomás and some fifty men raised a last wall in front of the inner gate. Thousands of torches were burning in the outer courtyard. Juan's horn was sounding.

And over a loudspeaker came Martín's voice:

"Surrender! Hand over Robert and the rebel witch, and the rest of you live! In Necrology there's work, there's bread! You'll live!"

Rosa let go of Robert's hand. She climbed the small tower above the inner gate. She switched on the fortress's old radio loudspeakers, the same ones through which Robert had broadcast hope into the desert for years. And she spoke. Not to Martín, nor to Juan. To those standing behind them.

"Listen to me! My name is Rosa. I was born in the third mine of Necrology. Many of you were born there too. I know, because I recognise your faces. I know you, Raúl: your wife was called Elena and she died when the ventilation collapsed. I know you, Bea: you lost your sister in my arms. They're promising you bread. Remember? They promised us the same bread. And our children died in the dark."

A murmur rose in the outer courtyard. The torches mingled.

"Inside these walls," Rosa went on, her voice trembling but not breaking, "the children go to school. Tomatoes grow in the gardens. Nobody is anybody's master. I didn't believe it. I didn't believe an idiot who dreamed, alone, of a fortress in the middle of the desert. But that man, one rainy night, when nobody believed him, came for me. And I loved him. And with him I loved this place. You can love it too. Who are you holding your weapons for? For us, or for the people who will put you back in the mines?"

There was a long, terrible silence.

Then, in the middle of the outer courtyard, a man threw his torch on the ground. Another, his rifle. One of Juan's men threatened an officer in a Necrology uniform with fire, and that old miner punched the officer. Chaos broke out inside the army: the former slaves on one side, the savages loyal to Juan and the Necrology mercenaries on the other.

But Juan's men were still many. And Martín, roaring like a beast, was driving the most loyal part of the army towards the inner gate: the armoured trucks and hundreds of motorbikes. They had filled the narrow passage between the outer and inner rings, the stone corridor that Luis called "the throat". The inner gate shook under the first blow.

Just then Luis appeared beside Robert.

Since the night of the coup he had hardly spoken. In his hand he held a large folded plan, and a torch hung from his belt. Around his neck, once again, was that colourful scarf.

"Robert," he said. "When I drew the roof of the throat, I added something. I told nobody. Under the keystone of the arch there's a cavity. Inside are the last boxes of explosives we brought from the mine when we built the fortress. In case the fortress ever fell, so the enemy couldn't get through to the inner ring. If we bring down the throat, half the army stays under the stones. The rest can't pass."

Robert looked at him.

"Can we set it off from a distance?"

"There was a fuse with a cable. But last night the drones destroyed the cable duct." Luis was silent for a moment. "Someone has to go down and set it off by hand. Through the maintenance passage in the roof of the throat. And that passage has no way back, because the keystone holds it up too."

Rosa turned towards them. She looked at Luis's face and understood.

"No," said Robert. "I'll go. I started this fortress..."

"You can't even walk, idiot," said Luis, smiling for the first time. A bitter smile, but a true one. "And I drew every step of that passage. I'll find it even in the dark. You dreamed this fortress, Robert. I only drew it. Now let me draw the last line of what I drew."

He turned to Rosa. Rosa's eyes were full of tears.

"I loved you badly, Rosa," Luis said quietly. "I loved you like someone who wants to win something, to own it. For you I nearly killed a man, and then I gave the keys to a traitor who sold us all. There's only one way to pay that debt. Let me love you well, for once. Without asking for anything."

Rosa embraced him. A brief, strong embrace that promised nothing.

"I forgive you, Luis," she whispered.

Luis closed his eyes, as if he had waited his whole life for that sentence. Then he held out his hand to Robert.

"Don't forget the mate. And let the children keep playing football. You were right. There are worse things in life."

Robert shook his hand. He could not speak.

Luis left.

Nobody remembered the following minutes clearly. The inner gate shook a second time, and then a third. The hinges creaked. Robert, beside Rosa, stood in front of the gate gripping his sword with both hands. Kennet had come down from the tower and taken his place on his left; Sasha, on his right. Oxana stood by a pot. Tomás had raised his scythe. Anselmo waited with a rifle in his hands, grumbling, "I'm a vet, this is really too much."

The gate broke at the fourth blow.

Martín was the first to come in. Behind him, hundreds.

"Dreamer!" he shouted.

And at that very instant the roof of the throat exploded.

The roar was as if the whole hill had split in two. The earth shook; Robert and Rosa were thrown to the ground. The arch of the stone corridor, that elegant arch Luis had drawn with such care, hung in the air for an instant and then, as if only now remembering its own weight, collapsed stone upon stone. Onto the armoured trucks, onto the motorbikes, onto Juan's most loyal warriors.

Martín stood on the threshold of the broken gate: he had got in, but the army behind him had vanished. Alone in the cloud of dust, bewildered, he looked back. Kennet knocked him down with one blow. Martín did not get up again; as he fell he had struck his head against the iron of the broken gate.

In the outer courtyard, those left on the other side of the throat at first stood frozen. Then they scattered. Juan's men fought among themselves, the former miners drove the Necrology officers into the desert, and the motorbikes fled in all directions. Juan himself, his necklace of bones broken and the yellow snake flag lying in the mud, vanished into the night with a handful of men.

Then came the silence. That strange, deafening silence that comes after battles.

Robert sat up. His wound had opened again; his shirt was soaked with blood. But he found Rosa's hand. And Rosa squeezed his.

"We're alive," said Rosa.

"We're alive," said Robert. And then, looking at the collapsed throat, at the place where a pale dawn was showing behind the cloud of dust: "Luis..."

Rosa rested her head on his shoulder and wept. For the first time, without limits, the way a little girl weeps.

From the cellars of the inner ring the babies, the mothers and the old began to come out. The people on the walls embraced each other. Kennet lifted Sasha off the ground and, without thinking, kissed her in front of the whole fortress. At first Sasha froze; then she grabbed him by the collar and kissed him back.

"I love you, you idiot bear," she said. "And I hate you for not noticing."

"I know," said Kennet. "Forgive me."

Oxana watched them and smiled; through tears, but truly. Then, to herself, softly, she began to hum a song. Nobody had ever heard it. It did not have a name yet.
