The coup came three days after the duel, on a moonless night.

In Luis's plans there was an old drainage tunnel that ran under the outer ring of the fortress and came out in a dry riverbed to the west. Luis had designed it as an escape route in case of siege and had told no one, not even Robert. After midnight, the first person to come through that tunnel was Jeni.

Behind her came about a hundred people, the same ones who had once left Our Utopia with her. But they were no longer disillusioned idealists wanting to build a "realistic utopia". They were armed. New rifles, night-vision binoculars, radios. Things nobody had in the desert.

Martín was waiting for them inside. Within an hour they had taken the armoury, two watchtowers and the main gate. They caught Kennet asleep, and four men could only overpower him by knocking him unconscious. They surprised Sasha bent over her account books. Oxana was tied up by Martín himself, who pulled the knots tighter than necessary.

"You called me a murderer," Martín whispered in her ear. "Now you'll see."

Anselmo fell at the hospital door under a rifle butt. But the hospital was empty.

That night, an hour before the coup, Luis had gone to the hospital. Rosa was dozing beside Robert's bed; when the door opened, she grabbed her pistol.

"Leave it," said Luis. His face was pale and his eyes red. "If you want to shoot me, shoot me later. Now listen. In an hour Martín and Jeni are going to take the fortress. And I helped them."

Rosa's pistol rose towards Luis's forehead.

"Why are you telling us?"

With trembling hands Luis held out a piece of paper. Martín had left it with him, out of carelessness or arrogance: the decoded text of a radio message.

*"The fortress will be ready at dawn on the third day. Gates open. Juan's army will secure the entrance. The Council of Necrology will take the installation as a surface base. The population will be classified as labour for the mines. Payment: two residences in Necrology and a seat on the council for Martín and Jeni."*

Rosa read it. The paper shook in her hand.

"The mines," she whispered. "They're going to take us back to the mines. The children too."

"I only..." Luis's voice broke. "I only wanted him to fall. Robert. In front of everyone, the way I fell. Martín promised me I'd govern the fortress. Until I read this I didn't know he'd sold us. I swear to you, Rosa. I didn't know."

Robert tried to sit up in bed; his stitches pulled and his face twisted with pain.

"How many men does he have?"

"About a hundred, with Jeni's people. And Necrology's weapons."

"And we have hundreds of people asleep," said Robert. "Unarmed."

"For now," said Rosa.

Luis led them to a small room in the foundations of the main tower that only he knew about. A secret chamber that opened behind a stone in the wall and was connected, through the ventilation ducts, to the whole fortress. They brought Robert down there with Rosa, half carrying him.

And the coup began.

When dawn came, all the people were gathered in the fortress square, under the shadow of the guns. Kennet, Sasha and Oxana were tied to a post. Martín stood on the stage; beside him, Jeni.

"People of Our Utopia!" shouted Jeni. "The dreamer's dream is over! From now on this fortress will be governed realistically. We will introduce a currency. Whoever works gets paid; whoever doesn't work gets nothing. No more free soup!"

"And order," Martín added. "Robert promised you peace. I promise you security. Anyone who knows where Robert is hiding, speak now. Anyone who doesn't will be considered as guilty as he is."

The crowd froze. Nobody spoke.

In the secret room, listening to the square through the grille of a ventilation duct, Robert heard his enemies' voices as if they came from a dream. His fever had risen; the wound was about to become infected. His head was spinning and the vision returned: torches, motorbikes, yellow flags. This time it was very close. Two days away.

Rosa threw cold water in his face.

"Stay with me, Robert."

"The army," Robert murmured. "It's on its way. Two days."

Luis was sitting in a corner, surrounded by a pile of plans. Rosa turned to him.

"You drew this fortress," she said. "Now show us how to take it back."

Luis raised his head. For the first time Rosa was looking at him not with hatred, but expecting something. And to Luis, that meant more than a kiss.

The plan was simple, because there was no time for another. Luis drew the map of the ventilation ducts. At night Rosa crawled through one of them and found Anselmo and Tomás. Tomás gathered ten of Rosa's rebels and five former savages: men whom Martín had humiliated for years by calling them "dirty savages" and who now had no intention of forgiving him. Anselmo, for his part, poured two bottles of sleeping draught, a foul-smelling but effective veterinary brew, into the guards' coffee pot.

At dawn after the second night, while half the guards were snoring in the towers, Robert came out of the secret room.

Rosa had tried to stop him.

"You've got a fever, your stitches will open!"

"Only I can bring the people together, Rosa," said Robert. "They have to see me with their own eyes. See that I'm alive. That I'm not hiding."

Rosa looked at him for a long time. Then she loaded her own pistol.

"Then we go together."

When Robert climbed onto the little fountain in the middle of the square, the sun was just rising. During the night Tomás's men had gone from door to door whispering: *At dawn. In the square.* And the people had come. First one by one, then in a mass. With shovels, pot lids, rolling pins and scythes in their hands.

"People of Our Utopia!" Robert shouted. His voice was breaking, but it reached every corner of the square. "Martín and Jeni have sold you! To Necrology! To the mines! Your children too! Here is the proof!"

He held up the paper. Rosa, beside him, read the radio message aloud word by word. And Rosa's people, those who had escaped from the mines of Necrology, began to shout one by one. First with rage, then with horror, then like an oath:

"We'll never go back! Never!"

When Martín came running into the square with his armed men, he no longer faced a frightened flock, but a furious people. And half his guards were still asleep from Anselmo's brew.

"Shoot!" shouted Martín.

His men hesitated. Shooting at hundreds of people, at women, at children. Even mercenaries had a limit. That hesitation was enough. Tomás's men leapt on them from the rooftops. The crowd surged forward like a wave.

Rosa ran to the post where Kennet, Sasha and Oxana were tied and cut their ropes with her knife. As soon as he was free, Kennet hurled himself roaring like a bear at the nearest mercenary. Jeni, seeing her plan collapse, pulled out a pistol and aimed at Sasha; but Sasha, that quiet woman who had spent years bent over account books, brought a heavy notebook down on her wrist. The pistol fell to the ground. Together, Kennet and Sasha pinned Jeni to the ground.

"Poor wretches!" Jeni was shouting. "All of you! Stupid poor wretches!"

Oxana ran to hug Kennet. Sasha looked away for an instant. But Kennet gently moved Oxana aside, took Sasha's hand and said a single word:

"Thank you."

And Sasha, in the middle of all that chaos, almost burst into tears.

Martín, meanwhile, had drawn his sword and was forcing his way through the crowd towards Robert.

"Dreamer!" he shouted. "This time there's no woman to save you!"

Robert drew his scimitar. The wound burned like fire, and under his shirt the stitches were opening one by one. When he parried Martín's first blow, his knees buckled. At the second he staggered back. The third opened his shoulder.

Martín was a soldier: ruthless, experienced, rested. Robert was wounded, feverish, two days without sleep. The duel seemed over before it began.

Martín raised his sword for the final blow.

And a bullet struck the hilt squarely and sent the sword flying from his hand.

Rosa stood ten paces away, smoke rising from the barrel of her pistol.

"You were wrong, Martín," she said. "There's always a woman."

Martín looked at his bleeding hand, then at Rosa, then at the furious crowd surrounding him. And he turned and ran. Towards the main gate, through the crowd, not with the speed of a warrior but with that of a rat. Nobody could catch him. He went out through the gate, jumped onto a waiting motorbike and vanished into the desert.

Robert collapsed. Rosa ran to his side.

"We won," Robert whispered.

But when Kennet shouted from the top of the north tower, the horror in his voice silenced the whole square.

"Robert! Look at the horizon!"

And everyone looked. To the south, where the desert ended, a wall of dust was rising. From one end of the horizon to the other. From inside it came the drone of thousands of engines.

The vision was no longer a vision. They had come.
