The next morning Robert gathered the people in the square and told them about the army he had seen. The snake flag, the golden-gate flag of Necrology, the torches stretching to the horizon. Martín's name, however, he did not mention: he had no proof, only a dream, and accusing a man on the strength of a dream would have betrayed the world he wanted to build.

The crowd murmured uneasily. And Luis stepped forward.

"A dream," he said, in a cold, cutting voice. "Our leader faints at a concert, has a dream, and the next day we have to prepare for war. Is this a fortress or a fortune-teller's temple?"

Some laughed. Robert looked at Luis in surprise. In Luis's eyes there was something he had never seen before: a wound. And the rage of wounded animals.

"Luis," said Robert calmly. "Your problem isn't my dreams. We both know that."

Luis turned red as a tomato. He tore the scarf from his neck and threw it on the ground.

"You're right. My problem is you. I drew this fortress. Every stone, every arch, every staircase. And you, the man who dreams, took everything: the glory, the people's affection... and her. So I'm making you an old-fashioned proposal. A duel. Tomorrow at dawn. In this square."

The crowd fell silent at once.

"I'm a humanist, Luis," said Robert. "In this fortress nobody runs anybody through with a sword over a woman."

"Coward!" shouted Luis. "Your humanism is nothing but the shield of a man who's afraid of losing!"

Rosa pushed her way through the crowd and stepped between them.

"Enough!" Her voice split the square like a whip. "I am not a prize! In Necrology they weighed us like gold and sold us. Is that what I came here for? For two men to pull at me like a bone?"

Luis hesitated for an instant. But the rage inside him had already become a monster that thought with its own head.

"This isn't about you, Rosa," he said. "It's about my honour. If he doesn't accept, tomorrow I'll leave this fortress. And I'll take my plans with me. All the fortress's secrets. All its weak points."

It did not sound like a threat, but like a whisper. But Kennet's hand went by itself to the hilt of his sword. Robert looked at Luis for a long time.

"To first blood," he said at last. "When the first blood is drawn, it's over. And whoever wins, Rosa decides for herself."

"I accept," said Luis.

That night Rosa went to Robert's room. She came in without knocking.

"Idiot," she said. "You're an enormous, stubborn, dreaming idiot."

"I know."

"And if you die?"

"To first blood, Rosa."

"Today I looked that man in the eyes," said Rosa. "He won't stop at first blood."

Robert looked at her. Rosa's hands were trembling. For the first time those hands were trembling, hands that had not trembled even in the canyon, on the night of the rain.

"Are you afraid for me?" he asked, unable to believe it.

Rosa did not answer. She sat down beside him, rested her head on his shoulder, and they stayed like that until morning. Without speaking.

Dawn came red and cold. In the centre of the square a circle had been drawn in chalk. The whole fortress was there; nobody spoke. Kennet was to be the referee. Sasha waited with a stretcher next to Anselmo. Oxana stood to one side, her hands joined as if praying. Martín, though, was behind the crowd, arms folded, with a barely visible smile.

When Luis stepped into the circle, everyone was surprised. He had taken off his elegant shirt and his scarf and wore a tight black sweater. The sword he held was thin and long, like a fencing sword. And the way he held it was not an architect's, but that of a fencer who had trained for years.

"When I was young I fenced at a club," said Luis, seeing the surprise on Robert's face. "I competed in the national championship three times. I never told you, because you never asked. You never ask anybody anything, Robert. You only tell."

Robert felt those words more deeply than a sword blow.

Kennet raised his hand. And lowered it.

Luis attacked first, like a snake: fast, straight, almost invisible. Robert deflected it at the last instant with his scimitar; the clash of steel on steel rang across the square. Luis stepped back, circled Robert like a dancer and attacked again. One, two, three blows. Robert parried them all, but each one drove him back a step.

Robert's scimitar was heavy and powerful; Luis's sword was light and fast. Robert had learned to fight against savages, in the desert, in the mud; Luis, in a hall, with rules, in front of a mirror. And that morning, the rules were on Luis's side.

And Robert's head was spinning. That cursed dizziness; it had not entirely left him since the night of the concert. For an instant the square seemed to sway around him. Luis saw it.

The sword drew an arc in the air and grazed Robert's left arm. Blood sprang out. A cry rose from the crowd.

"First blood!" shouted Kennet. "It's over!"

But Luis did not stop.

His eyes clouded, panting, as if he had not heard Kennet's voice, he lunged once more. While Robert, surprised and defenceless, was clutching his arm, the sword went into his right side, just below the ribs.

For an instant Robert looked at Luis's face. In Luis's face there was no triumph, but horror; as if only now did he understand what he had done. He pulled the sword out. Robert fell to his knees. Hot blood ran from the open wound over the stones, which still held the cold of the night.

Kennet seized Luis like a bear and threw him to the ground. The crowd began to boil. And Rosa, with a speed nobody could have stopped, crossed the chalk line, entered the circle and knelt beside Robert.

"Anselmo!" she shouted. "Quickly!"

She pressed her hands to the wound; the hot blood ran between her fingers. She looked into Robert's face.

"Look at me," she said. "Don't you dare close your eyes. Do you hear me? Don't you dare."

"I'm... fine," whispered Robert. "First blood... I lost..."

"Be quiet."

Rosa stood up. Her hands were covered in blood. She looked at the crowd and then at Luis, writhing on the ground under Kennet. This time her voice did not shout; on the contrary, it was very low. But the whole square heard it.

"Yesterday I told you I wasn't a prize. That no duel, no sword, could decide for me. Now I make my own decision. With my own mouth."

She knelt beside Robert again and took his bloody hand in her own, bloody too.

"I choose this man. Not because he promised me a fortress. But because one rainy night, when nobody believed him, he came for me alone, through two hundred kilometres of mud. And because right now, even while he's bleeding, he's honest enough to admit that he lost."

You could have heard a pin drop in the square.

Kennet let go of Luis. Luis got up slowly. He looked at the bloodied sword in his hand as if it belonged to someone else. Then he laid it on the ground. He looked at the crowd; the crowd looked away. The children who used to wave at him hid behind their mothers' skirts.

Luis left the square without a word.

Anselmo and Sasha put Robert on the stretcher and carried him to the hospital. The wound was deep, but it had not touched any vital organ. Anselmo stitched it with his old hands, with the patience of a vet.

"Humans are animals too," he said as he tied the last knot. "But you're a particularly stubborn animal."

Rosa did not leave his side all night.

That night, in the old storeroom in the darkest corner of the fortress, Luis sat alone. On the table there was a bottle; everyone knew alcohol was forbidden in Our Utopia, but Luis always had a hidden shelf. The door creaked.

Martín came in.

"They threw you out like a dog, Luis," Martín said softly. "In the fortress you drew yourself. You dreamed these stones. He just gave speeches on top of them. And now he's got the woman too."

Luis did not raise his head.

"What do you want, Martín?"

"Only justice," said Martín. "And your plans. The secret passages. Nothing more."

Luis was silent for a long time. Then, very slowly, he opened the drawer of the table.
