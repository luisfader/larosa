Two months passed after the savages' attack and, for the first time, life in Our Utopia began to look like a real life. The wounded savages who chose to stay repaired the walls, worked in the vegetable gardens and taught the children trades. Rosa's rebels had taken charge of the greenhouses; Anselmo, opening the fortress's first henhouse, was as proud as a king at his coronation. The children played football in the square; Luis did not like it, and grumbled, "In my country there were wars over this game," but Robert told him, "Let them play, Luis. There are worse things in life."

Robert founded a school. Sasha would teach mathematics; Kennet, survival skills; Martín, physical education and martial arts. Rosa, after long negotiations, agreed to teach "History".

"What history?" asked Robert.

"The history of those who lost," said Rosa. "Everyone already knows the history of those who won."

One day Luis came to Robert's room with a thick notebook in his hand. He had written a "Manifesto of Humanism": a passionate text about the Renaissance, about human dignity and about the choice between the barbarity of individualism and the rebirth of a true humanism. Robert listened and liked it.

"You must read it in the square," he said.

"I will," said Luis. Then, on his way out, he looked through the window at Rosa, who was telling the children something in the courtyard. A long look, far too long. Robert did not notice.

Kennet and Oxana met every night in the north tower. Kennet told her about the constellations: about Orion, Orion's belt, their legends. On the first night Oxana had provoked him: "I hope nobody's been killed from this tower"; because on her first evening Martín had taken her up the south tower to show her the rocket launcher, boasting, "This is what we hit the savages with," and Oxana had shouted "Murderer!" and run away. That night Martín and Kennet came to blows in the tower; Kennet nearly went over the edge of the wall before Sasha arrived and separated them. Robert sentenced Martín to a month of night watch.

Martín did not forgive him. Not Robert, not Kennet, not Oxana.

Sasha saw everything and said nothing. One night she surprised Kennet in the corridor, just as he was about to knock on Oxana's door.

"Do you need help?" she asked him, with a very well hidden tremor in her voice.

"Yes," said Kennet innocently. "I want to talk to Oxana."

"Go to hell!" said Sasha, and turned on her heel and left.

Kennet stood frozen in the middle of the corridor. And for the first time, looking at Sasha's back, he began to understand something.

As for Robert and Rosa... there was something between them, but neither dared to give it a name. In the evenings they met in the tower, talked for hours and suddenly fell silent; and that silence weighed more than the words.

One night Robert took the little desert rose out of his pocket.

"You never threw it away?" said Rosa, surprised.

"I tried to throw it away. Twice. Once I threw it in the rubbish and then got up in the night to look for it."

Rosa laughed. It was the first time Robert had seen her laugh; a whole laugh, without limits, like a little girl's. His heart stopped for an instant.

"Robert," said Rosa, suddenly serious. "Why are you looking at me like that?"

"I don't know," said Robert sincerely. "Years ago I stopped looking at women. They all left. So I decided not to look. I felt no pain for any woman. Until that night of rain."

Rosa's gaze softened. She reached out and laid her hand on the desert rose, in Robert's palm.

"I had decided not to trust anyone either," she said. "After Necrology. And look: neither of us could keep our decision."

The day of the concert came. In the square they built a stage and hung lanterns. Nobody drank alcohol; everyone drank tea or the mate that Luis had taught them to love. All the inhabitants of the fortress were there: former soldiers, former savages, former slaves, children. Oxana stepped onto the stage and began to sing her song "You Are My Fortress". Her voice rang off the turquoise walls and rose to the stars.

Rosa was next to Robert. At one point, in the most beautiful part of the song, Robert took her hand. Rosa did not pull it away.

Luis was in the front row. He turned his head and saw the two hands intertwined. First he went pale, then red. When the song ended, amid the applause, he stood up and shouted over the crowd:

"I love you, Rosa!"

For a moment the square sank into silence. Then some laughed and others whistled. Rosa blushed; without letting go of Robert's hand, she lowered her head.

Just then Robert began to feel dizzy.

The ground was slipping from under his feet again. That familiar knife was driving into his temples. The noise of the crowd moved away and grew muffled. Robert let go of Rosa's hand, staggered a few steps back into a dark passage and collapsed. Nobody saw him; every eye was on Luis.

This time the vision was bigger. An army. Thousands of motorbikes, thousands of torches, a cloud of dust stretching to the horizon. And flags: a coiled snake on a yellow background. And beside it another flag, a golden gate on a black background: the coat of arms of Necrology. And in the middle of that army, on a motorbike, a familiar face: Martín. He was riding in through the open gates of the fortress.

Rosa realised that Robert was not beside her and ran to look for him. She found him lying on the ground, in the darkness of the passage.

"Robert!" She knelt and took his head in her lap. He was breathing, but his eyes were closed, and beneath the lids his pupils were moving as if in a nightmare. "Robert, wake up! Please!"

She shook him, called his name, patted his cheeks. Robert did not wake. And Rosa, between despair and something else, without thinking, leaned down and kissed him on the lips.

Robert's eyes flew open. The army, the flags, the torches dissolved like mist. Only Rosa's face remained, very close, wet with tears.

"What happened?" Robert murmured.

"You fell. I couldn't wake you. So I..."

She did not finish the sentence. There was no need.

"I saw an army, Rosa," said Robert, trying to sit up. "They're coming here. With the snake flag. And with the flag of Necrology. And someone was opening the gates for them from inside."

Rosa went white.

At the mouth of the passage, where the lantern light ended, stood Luis. He had followed Rosa. He had seen everything. The kiss, the tears, the hair falling over Robert's face. The love he had declared had turned into a joke inside that dark passage.

Nobody saw him. Luis turned in silence and was lost in the crowd. But that night, in his room, he spread the plans of the fortress on the table and looked at them until dawn. The passages nobody knew about. The doors nobody knew about.

That same night, while the fortress slept, Martín too slipped through the shadows towards the gate. Sasha saw him from her window and ran after him.

"Martín! Where are you going at this hour?"

"To a lonely place, to pray," said Martín. "You know Robert doesn't want religion here."

Sasha did not believe him. But she let him go. And afterwards she would never forgive herself.
