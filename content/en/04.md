In the desert, rain is as rare as a miracle and as heavy as a curse. That night the sky seemed to have decided to pour out at once all the water it had saved for three years. The drops drummed on the turquoise roofs of Our Utopia, and little streams ran through the courtyards. People had come out joyfully, and children were dancing in the mud. Sasha, recording in her notebook how the water tanks were filling, was almost smiling.

Robert, though, stood frozen at his window.

Towards midnight the first dizziness came. The ground seemed to slide from under his feet; a sharp pain had pierced his temples. He held on to the edge of the table. An image appeared before his eyes, as clear as a dream, as real as a memory:

A narrow canyon. Patched tents. Flames smelling of petrol that would not go out under the rain. Screaming children. And at the bottom of the canyon, her back against the rock, an empty pistol in her hand, a woman surrounded. A thick braid. Honey-coloured eyes.

"Rosa," said Robert aloud, surprising even himself.

The image vanished. Robert was left gasping. He tried to tell himself it was a nightmare, that it came from tiredness, from the sound of the rain. He no longer worried about any woman. He had been repeating that to himself for years. But his hands were shaking, and inside his heart, behind that steel door, something was beating its fists against the wall.

He grabbed his jacket, fastened his sword to his belt and ran down the stairs two and three at a time. In the courtyard he bumped into Kennet.

"Where are you going in this weather?"

"To the canyon. To the rebels' camp. The savages are attacking them."

Kennet looked at him in disbelief.

"How do you know? Did something come over the radio?"

"No. I know, that's all."

"Robert, that's two hundred kilometres away. In this rain the sand will have turned to mud. In an hour there'll be a flood. You can't go alone. At least wait while I gather the men..."

"I can't wait," said Robert. His voice was barer than Kennet had ever heard it. "When it's morning, take the men and follow me. I'm going now."

The old motorbike did not start on the first try. On the second it coughed. On the third it roared. Robert shot out of the fortress gate like a bullet and vanished into the night.

The journey was a nightmare. The beam of the headlight barely reached a few metres past the curtain of rain. The wheels skidded in the mud and the bike went over on its side twice. Dry riverbeds had become furious brown rivers; crossing one, the water rose to Robert's knees and nearly carried the bike away. Robert pressed on, shouting, swearing, praying like someone who had forgotten how to pray. A single word was beating in his heart: *in time, in time, in time.*

Three hours after midnight he reached the mouth of the canyon.

He had been right.

Flames were rising from inside the canyon; not even the rain could put them out, because the savages had soaked the tents in petrol. The roar of the engines, the women's screams and the gunfire mingled. On the attackers' backs there was a new emblem: a coiled snake on a yellow background. The man leading them stood on the seat of his motorbike on top of a rise: huge, bearded, with a heavy necklace of bones around his neck. Later Robert would learn that his name was **Juan**: Samuel's master, the chief of all the southern tribes.

Robert switched off his lights, put the bike in top gear and plunged into the canyon.

The savages only noticed him when he was among them. Robert swung his sword from the bike; he cut through one man's torch and another's chain. He saw Anselmo: the old man was pressing a child to his chest, hiding behind a rock. He saw Tomás, covered in blood, holding out alone with a scythe against three savages.

And he saw Rosa.

At the very bottom of the canyon, exactly as in the vision, her back was against the rock. Her pistol was clicking on empty. Blood ran from her forehead, mixing with the rain and dripping from her chin. Five savages surrounded her; they were in no hurry, laughing like wolves that had cornered their prey. Among them, his left arm still bandaged, was Samuel.

"That day you took my prey from me, witch," Samuel was saying. "Tonight it's time to pay."

Robert drove the bike straight at them.

The front wheel knocked two savages to the ground. Robert jumped from the seat, rolled once in the mud and got up with his sword. The third savage threw himself at him; Robert ducked, turned and struck the man's temple with the back of his scimitar. The fourth ran away.

Samuel was left.

"You!" Samuel shouted hoarsely. "The hero of the desert! You're alive?"

"That day I disappointed the vultures," said Robert. "Tonight I'll disappoint you."

The duel was short and merciless. Samuel was strong and furious, but his arm was wounded and the mud slowed him down. Robert, though, was clearer that night than he had ever been in his life: he saw every blow coming, he took every step like a dance. When Samuel's sword cut the air once more, Robert slipped beneath it, turned, and drove his scimitar through a gap in the spiked armour into the man's chest, up to the hilt.

Samuel sank to his knees in the mud. For a moment he looked at Robert; in his eyes there was no anger, but a strange astonishment.

"We... fought for the Great State," he whispered. "They promised us a world..."

Then he fell on his face and did not get up again.

Robert turned to Rosa. She had slid down to the foot of the rock, holding her side with one hand; blood was seeping between her fingers.

"You," said Rosa, unable to believe it. "What... what are you doing here?"

"I told you the gate would always be open to you," said Robert. "Since you didn't come to the gate, I brought the gate to you."

Rosa tried to laugh, but the laugh turned into a moan.

At the mouth of the canyon Juan's horn sounded; seeing Samuel fall, the savages were pulling back, but only to regroup. Robert knew what that meant: before dawn they would be back, in greater numbers.

Tomás limped over to them.

"I'll take the survivors to the mountains," he said, out of breath. "To the caves in the north. But she's wounded. She can't ride, she's losing blood."

Robert did not even think. He gathered Rosa into his arms and lifted her.

"Let me go," Rosa protested weakly. "I can't leave my people..."

"Your people don't want you dead," said Robert. "Tomás will take them. Then we'll find you. All of you."

Tomás looked at Rosa, then nodded.

"Go, Rosa. For once in your life, let someone else save you."

Robert sat her in front of him on the bike, wrapped her in his own jacket and tied her to himself with his belt. Rosa's head fell onto his shoulder. Her wet hair smelled of smoke and rain.

As the rain was about to stop and the first pale lights appeared over the desert, Robert saw the headlights of Kennet's men halfway along the road. Rosa came round for a moment.

"How did you know?" she whispered.

Robert did not answer. Because he did not know either. All he knew was that, that night, under the rain, the steel door of his heart had been torn off its hinges.
