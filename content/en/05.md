Rosa spent three days burning with fever. In the fortress's small hospital, with the last antibiotics that Sasha had counted and set aside, and with Robert's unbroken vigil. Robert slept in a chair by the bed, woke up, wet the cloth on her forehead, fell asleep again. One afternoon Kennet looked in at the door and withdrew in silence. Sasha folded her arms:

"I've never seen him look at a woman for so long."

"It's the first time a woman has let him look at her with her eyes closed," said Kennet, smiling. "Maybe that's why he dares."

On the morning of the fourth day Rosa opened her eyes, looked at the turquoise ceiling and then at Robert.

"I'm here?" she said. "Inside your... walls?"

"Yes."

"Where are my people?"

"Kennet and his men found them in the caves in the north. Thirty-two people alive. Anselmo too, and Tomás. They'll be here tomorrow."

A wave of relief crossed Rosa's face; but at once her jaw hardened.

"Thank you, Robert. As soon as I can stand, we're leaving."

Robert opened his mouth to say something and closed it again. Rejected. Again. But this time it did not hurt; or it hurt, and he felt it not as a wound but as a sign of being alive.

"As you wish," he said. "The gate is open. In both directions."

When Rosa healed, she did not leave. First she stayed because she could not walk. Then she stayed because the children of her people met a blackboard and chalk for the first time, in the fortress school. Then she stayed because Anselmo wept when he saw the greenhouses. And in the end she stayed for a reason she confessed to no one.

But staying did not mean becoming meek. Rosa questioned every corner of Our Utopia. In the assemblies she stood up to challenge Robert.

"Who makes the decisions here?"

"Everyone."

"Oh, really? At every assembly I see you talking and everyone else nodding."

A murmur rose in the hall. Some laughed, others got angry. Robert, to everyone's surprise, lowered his head.

"You're right. From now on, then, I won't be the first to speak."

For a moment Rosa was thrown; she had prepared for a fight and found no shield in front of her.

In the evenings they began to climb to the top of the main tower. By chance, they both said. There Rosa, looking at the darkness spreading over the whole valley, told him about Necrology: about its pools that shone like jewels, its marble staircases and the well-mannered people who saw the children in the mines and pretended not to see them. Robert told her about his mother. About his first love. About all the women who had called him a dreamer.

"Maybe they were right," said Rosa.

"Maybe."

"But look," said Rosa, pointing at the windows lighting up one by one below. "The dreamer's dream is now the shelter of hundreds of people. The realists didn't do that."

Robert looked at her. Rosa looked away.

That night, as they were going down the stairs, Robert said in his own language, almost without noticing:

"Buenas noches, querida."

Rosa stopped on a step.

"What does that mean? *Querida?*"

"*Canım,*" he translated. "My dear."

"Ah." Rosa thought for a moment, tried the word on the tip of her tongue, with the wrong stress: "*Querida*... That's pretty. Thank you." And before going out of the door, biting her lips so as not to laugh: "Say it and go, then. Go."

* * *

Rosa had a strange way of speaking. When she was going to say something important, the words came out one at a time, as if she were releasing each one separately into the wind. Robert understood it much later: Rosa could only say important things like that, in pieces.

One night, after a long, bad assembly, Robert was sitting in the tower without saying a word.

"Tell me," said Rosa.

"It's nothing."

"If you don't... tell me... the problem..." Rosa took a deep breath. "I'll go crazy. Right now. Really."

Robert laughed in spite of himself.

"You're good for me, you know?"

"Really?"

"Yes. Sometimes I think that... maybe we're soulmates."

"I know," said Rosa. She said it so calmly that Robert looked at her in surprise.

"Maybe we can never be together," said Robert. "You have your oath. The walls. Leaving."

Rosa looked at the dark valley for a long time. Then she spoke, word by word:

"I... will always... love you. One way or another. Don't forget it. Okay?" She was silent for a moment. "It's not... a passing thing!"

"Are you sure?"

"Yes. Very sure. Never... before... have I been so sure."

"Maybe in another life," said Robert.

"Maybe. In another life."

"Then I'll start believing in another life. So I can be with you."

Rosa turned her face towards him and laughed that whole, childlike laugh.

"You're very, very, very, very *tatlı*!" she said, in Turkish: sweet.

"*Tatlı mısın?*" Robert repeated, then frowned. "I mean... *tatlı mıyım?* Am I sweet?"

"Yes," said Rosa. "You're sweet. And your Turkish is terrible."

Robert's face darkened again. Rosa touched his arm.

"Smile a little."

Robert smiled.

As they said goodbye at the top of the stairs, Robert was going to say "Good night" as always. But this time he wanted to be careful; he wanted to respect Rosa's oath, her distance.

"Good night, *amiga mía*. My friend."

Rosa turned round. Her eyes were shining in the dark.

"You don't have to call me friend, Robert. Please, understand me. We don't have to call each other that. It... hurts me. Call me Rosa. That's enough."

"My Rosa."

"But you never find the middle ground!" Rosa laughed, then lowered her voice. "All right. I'm telling you for the last time, but... my Robert."

And she vanished into the darkness.

The next morning they crossed paths in the courtyard, among the water buckets.

"Good morning," said Robert. "How are you?"

"*Así así,*" said Rosa, so-so, with words she had stolen from his language and a little pride. "*¿Vos?*"

"Bad."

"Me too," Rosa confessed. "I couldn't sleep all night."

Robert put the bucket down on the ground.

"I'm not your master, Rosa. Here nobody owns anybody. But your soul... your soul is mine."

Rosa looked at him for an instant. Then she picked up her bucket and walked on. Three steps further, without turning round:

"Yes. It's true."

A week later Kennet knocked at the door. This time he was not soaked, but his face was as dark as the night of the rain.

"Robert, we have a problem. The savages have found out about us. Juan wants to avenge Samuel's death. And he knows we're hiding the rebels who escaped from the canyon."

Robert gathered the most trusted people of the fortress in the main hall: Kennet, Sasha, Martín, Luis. Rosa and Tomás came too, uninvited. Nobody dared to send them away.

"By my calculations," said Sasha, leafing through her notebook, "the savages' resources aren't enough for a long siege. Thanks to the wells, we can hold out for six months."

"And I'll prepare the traps," said Martín, his eyes shining like a child's in front of a toy. "Let them dance a bit before they get near our walls."

"Maybe we should try talking first," said Kennet. "Without shedding blood."

Kennet and Martín went to the savages' camp with a white flag. The camp was on a wooded hill; fires burned all around it and in the centre rose a large wooden building decorated with spoils. On the walls there were shields with the badge of a famous football club, posters of a charismatic long-haired politician with sideburns, and flags reading "Don't tread on me" under the coiled snake on a yellow background. The air smelled of fernet and grilled chorizo; a huge dollar sign flew from the flagpoles.

The negotiation lasted for hours and went nowhere.

"Hand over the rebel witch," Juan said at last. "And Samuel's murderer. Then we'll talk."

Kennet and Martín came back empty-handed.

Robert did not give up. This time he took Luis, because Luis, to everyone's surprise, had confessed that he had once lived among those people.

"Before the Catastrophe they were ordinary people," Luis said on the way. "Football and politics. They argued about matches for hours, they criticised governments, but they didn't lift a finger to change anything. Then they voted for a leader who promised to end inflation and give the country back its old glory. That leader sold everything. And then the Catastrophe came."

Robert climbed onto a rock in the middle of the camp and spoke. He spoke of peace, of shared work, of how the gates of Our Utopia could open for them too.

Juan burst out laughing.

"Are you offering us a place in your socialist project? Are you kidding us?"

The camp exploded in loud laughter. As Robert and Luis withdrew, Juan shouted after them:

"Hey, you, traitor! You'll pay for everything!"

Luis went white. *So they recognised me*, he thought. He did not say a word the whole way back.
