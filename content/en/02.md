When Robert opened his eyes, there was a patched tent canvas above him. Inside it smelled of thyme, blood and wood smoke. His back was burning, and his ribs stabbed him like a knife with every breath. At his bedside, an old man with a white beard down to his chest was cleaning the wound.

"Don't move," said the old man. "Two of your ribs are cracked, and your back looks like earth that's taken a rain of chains. But you'll live. My name is Anselmo. Before the Catastrophe I was a vet. Humans are animals too, so you can trust me."

The camp was set up at the bottom of a narrow canyon, between two walls of rock leaning towards each other like two boats. There were about twenty tents, a well, sunshades made of sheets and a small vegetable garden. About fifty people lived there: women, men, a few children. They all had the same thing in their faces: the look of people who had once been in chains.

Robert learned their story on the third evening, by the fire. Rosa told it.

"In the north, under the mountains, there's a city," she said, looking into the flames. "It's called **Necrology.** Before the Catastrophe, the rich bought themselves underground shelters. An underground paradise with pools, gardens and concert halls. But even paradise needs someone to clean it. To repair the ventilation, to work in the kitchen, to dig coal in the mines. We were those people. They promised us 'a safe future'. A utopia."

She threw a branch onto the fire.

"Two years ago we rose up. Most of us died. We managed to escape. Since then we've lived in the desert. Belonging to no one."

Robert listened in silence. Then, straightening up as far as his ribs allowed, he spoke:

"That's exactly why you should join me. I don't want to build a place like Necrology. I want a fortress with no money and no masters, where everyone shares the work. High walls, watchtowers, water tanks. The savages will never be able to touch you again. It will be called Our Utopia. *Ours.* Nobody's estate."

The people around the fire looked at each other. A tall man with one ear cut off, Tomás, laughed first, then spat.

"Utopia. I never wanted to hear that word again. It was written on Necrology's brochures too: 'A New World, A New Utopia.'"

Old Anselmo shook his head.

"Walls, son, are things that protect you on the first day and imprison you on the second."

Robert turned to Rosa. She said nothing for a long time. At last, slowly:

"You're a good man, Robert. I can see that. But fortresses built by good men fall into bad men's hands more easily. We survive by moving. Today we're here, tomorrow in another canyon. No one can besiege us, because we have no place to be besieged."

"And no one protects you," said Robert. "Your children will never go to school. You'll never plant a tree and eat its fruit."

Rosa's eyes flashed for a moment; Robert understood that he had touched her heart. But Rosa stood up.

"Let the council vote."

The vote was short. Five people raised their hands; everyone else shook their heads. Rosa did not raise her hand. But you could not say she had kept it down either; she had folded her arms across her chest and turned her gaze into the darkness.

Robert stayed in the camp for another week. Anselmo bandaged his wounds, and the children taught him an old game, a kind of hopscotch drawn in the sand. He rarely talked with Rosa; and when they did talk, they argued. About money, about walls, about human nature.

"Are people good or bad?" Rosa asked one night.

"Both," said Robert. "That's why you need rules, but not masters."

"Who makes the rules?"

"Everyone together."

"Everyone together," Rosa repeated, not mockingly, but as if tasting the word. "A pretty fairy tale."

On the morning of the seventh day they brought Robert's motorbike to the mouth of the canyon. Tomás had taken off the chain and straightened the wheel. There was fuel in the tank, and water and dried meat in the saddlebag.

"We healed you because we're human," said Rosa. "We're letting you go because we're free. Don't forget either."

She left something in his palm: a small crystal of sand and gypsum, opened in layers like a flower. A **desert rose.**

"The desert makes it over centuries, with wind and salt," she said. "Nobody builds it. It happens on its own. Maybe your utopia is like that too."

Robert did not know what to say. He had been rejected again; but this time with a gift. That hurt even more.

"The gate of the fortress will always be open to you, Rosa."

"I don't need a gate," said Rosa. "Go now."

Robert started the engine. As he left the canyon he looked once in the rear-view mirror. Rosa was still there, arms folded across her chest, a silhouette growing smaller behind the cloud of dust. Robert turned his eyes to the road and swore he would never look back again.

But he did not throw away the desert rose.
