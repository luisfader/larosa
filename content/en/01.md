Robert's motorbike, that old monster from the twentieth century, was tearing through the scorching silence of the desert with a muffled roar. He was thirty-eight; a man of medium height, with a short, untidy beard and a carefully trimmed moustache. His face had a hard but equally kind expression. That expression misled people who did not know him: they thought him either too hard or too soft.

Three years had passed since the Great Catastrophe. The shelter he had built in advance had saved him; the cities had not been saved. Behind him there were only collapsed motorways full of sand, skyscrapers whose skeletons bleached in the sun, and the memory of what had once been called "progress". Even so, Robert was chasing a dream: to build a fortress city where people could live without fear, without greed and without weapons. He had already given it a name: **Our Utopia.**

His mother's voice still rang in his ears. "You can't change the world, son. There's no such thing as utopia." She had not even been able to explain what a utopia was. That day Robert had smiled, hurt: "There is. And I'm going to build it."

Things had never gone well for him with women. The girl he loved when he was young told him, "You're a dreamer, and I want to live," and left. Those who came after said more or less the same sentence; some more kindly, some more cruelly. At some point Robert had closed the door himself. He expected no mercy and showed none. He had sealed his heart like the steel door of a shelter, and he did not complain about it. At least, that was what he told himself.

In the afternoon, when the sun stood at its highest, he saw a well among the reddish rocks. Beside the well, a woman who had covered her face against the dust with a shawl was watering two tired horses. Robert switched off the engine. The woman raised her head and lowered the shawl.

She had dark brown hair, tangled by the wind, falling over her shoulder in a thick braid. She had sunburned skin, sharp cheekbones and honey-coloured eyes that seemed to read people from the inside. She wore a pistol at her waist and a short rifle on her back. In her look there was neither fear nor curiosity; only an attention that measured and weighed.

"Did you come for water, or for trouble?" the woman asked.

"For water," said Robert. "And maybe a little to talk. I haven't talked to anyone in a long time."

"Talking is expensive in the desert," said the woman. Even so, she pushed the bucket towards him. "Drink. Then go. This area isn't good for lonely travellers like you."

Robert drank. He asked her name.

"Rosa."

"Robert. I want to build a fortress, Rosa. A place where people can live safely again. Where no one is a master and no one is a slave..."

Rosa let out a short, dry laugh.

"Everyone wants to build a fortress. Then the fortress gets a gate, the gate gets a guard, and the guard gets a master. Go, Robert. Go west, towards the mountains. The savages roam in the south."

She took her horses' reins and disappeared among the rocks. Robert watched her go, angry with himself. *The same thing again*, he thought. *You're rejected before you even open your mouth.* He got on his bike and, out of sheer stubbornness, rode south.

An hour later the dunes flattened and the sand turned into level, hard ground. Just then a cloud of dust rose on the horizon. At first he thought it was a sandstorm. Then he heard the engines: dozens of engines howling like hungry animals.

The savages burst out from behind the rocks. Their faces were covered with masks and rags; on their worn leather jackets hung spoils from past wars: teeth, feathers, metal spikes. They carried knives, chains and clubs. In front of them rode a man as big as a mountain of muscle, in spiked armour decorated with runic signs. His eyes were darkness and hunger. The others were shouting his name: **Samuel**.

Robert turned the bike to escape, but the back wheel got tangled in a chain. The bike was thrown and he rolled across the sand. When he got up he had his sword in his hand: an elegant scimitar with a leather-wrapped hilt and a curved, double-edged blade, as if it had stepped out of a medieval legend.

He ducked the first attacker's club and struck his wrist, sending the weapon flying. He wounded the second in the shoulder. The third came from behind and hit him across the back with a chain; Robert dropped to his knees. His mouth filled with blood and sand.

Samuel got off his bike and came closer with heavy steps.

"A hero," he said in a mocking voice. "Lonely heroism in the desert. We love it. The bike is ours, the water is ours, the sword is ours too. And you belong to the vultures."

Robert tried to get up. Samuel's knife rose in the air and flashed for a moment in the sun.

A gunshot echoed off the rocks.

The knife fell from Samuel's hand into the sand; the man stared in astonishment at his bleeding arm. The second bullet pierced the fuel tank of the savage's bike next to him. A dozen riders came down over the rocks; some with rifles, some with bows, some with farmers' scythes. On the leading horse, her braid flying in the wind, was Rosa.

"Let him go, Samuel!" she shouted. "This sand is our land!"

"Rosa!" Samuel spat. "Rebel witch!"

There was a short, savage clash. Rosa's people squeezed the savages from both sides. Rosa rode her horse in front of Robert, leaned down from the saddle and grabbed him by the collar.

"Get up! It's far too early to die."

The last thing Robert saw was Samuel jumping onto his bike with his wounded arm and fleeing with his men into the cloud of dust. And Rosa's face, very close, furious and worried.

"I told you to go west, you idiot," said Rosa.

Then everything went dark.
