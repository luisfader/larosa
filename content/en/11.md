It took three days to find Luis's body. It lay at the very top of the rubble of the throat, just beneath the keystone of the arch. His face was turned not towards the stones but upwards, towards the sky. In his hand he still held a pencil, and on his chest lay a folded sheet of paper: the drawing of a new arch to replace the throat. He had drawn it in his last minutes, in the dark, by the light of his torch. Underneath, three words were written:

*"Make it wider."*

They buried him beside the main tower, at the edge of the square. That night, in the silence that follows death, Oxana sang the song that had been born on its own; now it had a name: "The Architect's Song". The children left a football beside the grave. Kennet left a cup of mate. Rosa untied the colourful scarf from her neck (the one Luis had thrown down in the square on the morning of the duel, and which Rosa had kept ever since without telling anyone) and tied it to the headstone.

Robert did not speak. He stood by the grave for a long time. Then he only said:

"I never asked him anything. He was right. From now on I'll ask. Everyone."

The new arch was finished a year later: following Luis's last drawing, wider. By unanimous decision, the people named the tower rising above it **Luis Tower**.

The day after the battle, they put Jeni on a motorbike with water and food for a week and took her out of the fortress. Robert refused to execute her and refused to imprison her; this time the people agreed with him. As she went out through the gate, Jeni turned and shouted:

"Sooner or later your utopia will sink!"

"Maybe," said Robert. "But until it sinks, we'll learn to swim."

Two weeks after the war, Sasha gathered the whole fortress in the main hall. She still had that notebook in her hand, but this time its columns held not water and grain but other things.

"We have to accept it," she said. "Someone, someone from Necrology, gives the savages weapons and drones. They use them against us like a tool. And they'll try again. Because this fortress proves their greatest fear: that a life without masters is possible. We are no longer the project of a handful of romantics. We are a real alternative to post-apocalyptic chaos. And a real alternative needs institutions. An assembly. Written rules. And a defence."

A murmur rose in the hall. Everyone turned to Robert; out of old habit, they waited for him to speak first.

Robert, instead, looked silently at Rosa.

Rosa stood up.

"My people," she said, "swore for two years never to go inside any walls. Because they taught us that walls imprison. But I've learned something: what turns walls into a prison isn't the stones. It's who holds the key to the gate. If we all hold the key together..." She stopped, looked at Robert and smiled. "Then maybe it deserves its name. Our Utopia."

At the first assembly they elected eleven people. Sasha, for the economy. Kennet, for defence. Anselmo, for being the oldest and because nobody could say no to him. Tomás, as the representative of the former savages and former miners who had joined the fortress. Oxana, with an invented post "for culture and joy". And Rosa, with more votes than anyone.

Robert, however, did not stand.

"I dreamed," he said, when Rosa looked at him with questioning eyes. "Now I have to leave the governing to those who don't dream of governing. I'll teach history at the school. Not the history of those who won, but that of the dreamers."

Rosa laughed. With that whole, limitless, childlike laugh.

Kennet and Sasha were married on the first day of spring, in the square, in front of the whole fortress. Anselmo married them, saying, "I'm a vet, but human beings are animals too." Oxana sang "You Are My Fortress", and this time she found someone to dedicate it to with pride: Tomás. But that is another story.

And one night, exactly a year after the war, it rained again in the desert.

Robert, standing at the window, listened to the drops beating on the turquoise roofs. He did not feel dizzy. No vision came. His heart was calm. Just for an instant, out of old habit, he waited for a premonition. Nothing came.

"What are you waiting for?" asked Rosa behind him, from the bed.

"Nothing," said Robert, and realised it was true. "For the first time, I'm not waiting for anything. Everything is here."

Rosa got up, came over and rested her head on his shoulder. Her wet hair no longer smelled of smoke; only of soap and sleep.

Robert took the little desert rose out of his pocket. That crystal he had carried for years, that he had twice thrown in the rubbish and twice recovered. He set it on the windowsill, between the rain and the light.

"You once told me nobody built it," he said. "That wind and salt work for centuries and it opens on its own. That maybe my utopia would be like that too."

"I was wrong," said Rosa. "This fortress didn't open on its own. A dreamer dreamed it, an architect drew it, an accountant calculated it, a tracker protected it, hundreds of people built it. And one man stayed beneath his own arch to save it."

She fell silent. Outside, the rain ran down the turquoise walls and fell on the gardens, on the greenhouses, on Luis's grave, on the stones of the new arch.

"But love," Rosa said, very softly. "Maybe love does open on its own. Like the desert rose. Without anyone building it. Just waiting for the rain."

Robert kissed her. And this time, for the first time in his life, he was not afraid of being rejected.

*The End.*
